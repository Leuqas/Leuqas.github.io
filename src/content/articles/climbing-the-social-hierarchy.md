> "It wasn't even a ladder to begin with. Just now have I came to the realization that it was a running treadmill."

&emsp;**Social status**—an idea I once frowned upon. Moreover, the fact that people tried to climb this so-called ladder baffled me. I used to believe that gaining friends—or to a greater extent, popularity—should come naturally, as if it was just bound to happen to certain people. Maybe because of the way they talk, their looks, or how they approach things. I found the concept that people climbed this, even strategizing each action just for a small boost, ridiculous. Until, of course, I found myself doing it.

&emsp;At first, I was clueless, yet satisfied. Despite not having much of a social standing, nothing really mattered. In fact, it never even occurred to me that it was normal to talk to a classmate beyond the classroom. I saw my seatmate as nothing more than a locked character, confined to their chair. I was well content going home after class to spend time with myself doing things I labeled as hobbies. I assumed it was leisure; the idea of having to worry and adjusting plans to make time for strangers you're well to, I considered a hassle. But the longer I stayed in this bubble, the more I felt that something was missing. I couldn't name it at first, but I sure knew there was a gap somewhere.

&emsp;After years of being stuck in this my-life-revolving-around-myself abyss, I sensed a buildup of jealousy. As if all of a sudden, I now couldn't comprehend how I lived all these years without a group of people to cling to, seemingly as if it were such a foreign concept. I now look for this hassle I once avoided. I gave this whole concept a name: social hierarchy. And I was determined to climb it.

&emsp;I started to notice that people tend to gravitate towards certain individuals, usually the ones who walk in front of the group in the hallway. As if giving them the kind of treatment that I would've considered special, a normal predicate for others around them. Somehow, their opinions stood out amongst many others, such that whenever they spoke, people around them almost naturally would give all their care. They have this strange influence, where people by default would shift their perspectives the moment they voice theirs. I assumed these were the people high up in the social hierarchy.

&emsp;Then there are the people who attach themselves to these “higher-ups”—the ones who give them all this attention, ultimately building them up and reinforcing their status. And despite them not being the receiver of these prerogatives, they are still within the bounds of a circle—a circle that is composed of people that are around each other; I take it as a given that they are the ones lower than the higher-ups, presumably the middle of the hierarchy ladder.

&emsp;My attention then shifted to the people just lowly striding by the classroom, the ones who weren't part of any clear social group, yet they did not seem to be bothered by it. For what they show, they seem to be satisfied with what people had to offer them. I was in awe—how come are they so unconcerned? Asking this question came a reality that I overlooked the fact I am one of them.

&emsp;“Reality hit me like a truck!” A statement only now do I see the full spectrum of. From then on, I started acting on something I once dismissed. I began weighing every word, heavily thinking about the outcome of each action I took. I mimicked the way people around me spoke, the way they dressed, the way they reacted to certain things, even the way they laughed, all to try and fit in. And soon enough, I started noticing a change in how people acted around me, almost amusing me on how easy it was. It did not even feel like a hassle, because with the clarity I had on where I stood, my actions felt necessary.

&emsp;By the time I reached a comfortable spot towards my made-up scheme, I realized that it was not the thing I strived for at all. Looking back, I realized that even before putting a name to this whole scheme, I was already participating in it. The people I once looked up to—even deeming them untouchable—had their own insecurities, just as much as mine. On the other hand, the ones who I thought had no place up the ladder, actually had a life outside, beyond what I thought of what composes it all. It appears that life is more than just a hierarchy.

&emsp;The farther I climbed, the more cracks I saw on what I once thought was the pedestal. It wasn't all about who walked in front of the group, about who was the loudest, about whose words stood out the most, or even who got the most attention. 
> It wasn't even a ladder to begin with. Just now have I came to the realization that it was a running treadmill. 
Even those I once envied were still running—just more visibly, more desperately. And that’s when it clicked: the climb had no end.

&emsp;As pathetic as it may seem, the journey wasn't worth it. I come back to what my opinion on this topic was—it truly is baffling. Because at the end of the day, no one is handing out medals on who has the most friends. I feel regretful about how I spent my time. I mourn the years I spent chasing a place in a ladder that never even existed.

And as junior high ends, I don’t regret being unseen—**I regret ever thinking I had to be.**

&emsp;Because truthfully, the biggest change wasn't on how I saw myself, but how I perceived others. The seatmates I sat beside, whose stories ended as our teacher dismissed us, turned out to have so much more. They weren't just confined to their chairs, they were characters with narratives of their own—the only problem being I was too scared to approach them.

&emsp;I had once thought that friendships were supposed to be handed to me, or not at all. Now I realize that they don't simply appear; they grow, only when I'm willing to reach out and plant the seed myself. I failed to see the need for initiative: I have to say hi first, ask people what they brought for lunch, or even subtly bring up the homework that I did not do, all to start a conversation. It's awkward and uncomfortable, and the thought of being ignored is absolutely paralyzing. But looking back, my own past proves that nothing will change if I don't act first.
