I used to think that uncertainty was just a lack of information. If someone did something thoughtful, for example, then the only thing left to do was find out why. We could ask, observe, or wait for more clues. Until then, the meaning was simply missing.

But I noticed that people do not always wait. We fill the missing parts ourselves. A message goes unanswered, and we start weighing every word we sent. Someone remembers a detail we mentioned weeks ago, and suddenly we wonder whether it meant more than we thought. Nothing about the event has changed. Somehow, our experience of it has.

I found the concept of Schrödinger’s cat useful for thinking about that gap, though only as a metaphor. In 1935, physicist Erwin Schrödinger described a thought experiment in which a cat is sealed in a box alongside a mechanism linked to a quantum event. In the simplified version, the event’s outcome determines whether the cat lives or dies. Before the box is opened, the quantum description involves a superposition of possible outcomes.

Schrödinger was not proposing that ordinary cats literally spend their day being both alive and dead in the familiar sense. The thought experiment was meant to expose the difficulty of carrying quantum ideas about superposition into the world of everyday objects, and to challenge simple accounts of what measurement means. Physicists still interpret measurement in different ways. The box is a question about physics, not proof that looking at something makes it real.

That distinction matters. Ordinary uncertainty is often just ordinary uncertainty: we do not know what happened, or what someone intended. Quantum superposition is a technical feature of a mathematical theory. Human attention does not put a person’s feelings into a quantum state, and a delayed reply does not require an interpretation of quantum mechanics. The comparison ends there.

Still, I kept thinking about what happens in the space before the box opens. Not because all possibilities are secretly true, but because we can imagine several of them at once. We may hope that a gesture means affection, suspect that it means apology, or decide that it was only politeness. These explanations exist in our minds, where they can affect how we feel before we have enough reason to choose between them.

That is where the rose enters. A rose is a flower; its petals do not contain a label explaining what the giver intended. In one situation, it might be an apology. In another, a thank you, a sign of affection, or simply a flower someone liked enough to give away. The rose remains the same object, but the circumstances around it change what it means to the people involved.

I started calling this idea a Schrödinger’s rose: not a flower that is literally several things at once, but a gesture whose meaning has not yet settled for the person receiving it. The rose is in the space between the event and its interpretation. And unlike a sealed box, that space is shaped by the person looking at it.

We bring our past experiences with us. If people have surprised us kindly before, we may be more willing to see kindness in a new gesture. If we have been misunderstood or disappointed, we may look for the catch. Neither response tells us exactly what the other person meant. It tells us something about the observer, too.

At first, I thought the problem was that people overthink. Maybe we could avoid confusion by deciding what a gesture meant and moving on. But that can be its own kind of overthinking: treating a guess as a fact because uncertainty feels uncomfortable. A thoughtful gesture does not automatically prove a hidden intention. And the fact that someone meant well does not decide how the gesture will feel to someone else. Meaning and intention can meet, but they are not the same thing.

The answer cannot be to leave everything ambiguous forever. In a real relationship, clarity matters; no one should have to decode another person indefinitely. But there is a difference between demanding certainty before we have it and allowing ourselves a little time to understand what we know. We can notice what happened without pretending we know the whole story.

I used to believe that the meaning of an event was waiting somewhere outside us, like an answer we could uncover if we looked hard enough. Now I think interpretation is part of the event’s life. It begins with what someone does, but it also passes through the history, hopes, and assumptions of the person who receives it. We can be mistaken about intention and still be honest about what something meant to us.

Maybe that is why I like the rose as an image. It does not solve uncertainty. It gives it a shape we can hold in mind, while reminding us that context matters. An idea can remain on a page for a while, and still be waiting for the right context to mean something else.

The rose is not both apology and affection in the way a quantum system can be described as a superposition. It is a rose, and we are people trying to understand one another with incomplete information. Sometimes we will ask. Sometimes we will be told. And sometimes, before either happens, we will notice how much of the answer we supplied ourselves.
